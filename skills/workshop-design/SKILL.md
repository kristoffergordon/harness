# Workshop design

A workshop is worth running only if it produces something a meeting or a document could not. Design backwards from what the group must leave with.

## 1. Frame it

Establish, asking at most three questions and only for what is missing:

- **Outcome:** the decisions, artifacts or shared understanding the group must leave with. At most three. A topic is not an outcome ("discuss the AI roadmap") but a result is ("a ranked shortlist of three use cases with owners").
- **Participants:** who, how many, their seniority and relationships, and who has the final say.
- **Constraints:** duration, in person or remote, room, tools, and what is already decided.

## 2. Challenge the brief

Say so plainly if any of these applies:

- The outcome could be reached with a shorter meeting, interviews or a document. Recommend that instead.
- The group is too large or too mixed for the outcome (decision-makers and detail-owners together, or more than about 12 people for hands-on work).
- The time is too short for the ambition. A half-day holds about two real outcomes, not five.
- A decision is expected that the people in the room cannot make.

## 3. Build the agenda

A table: **time, minutes, purpose, method, output**.

- Every block has an output. If a block has none, cut it or merge it.
- Minutes must add up to the session length. Include a welcome and framing block, breaks (10 minutes per 90 of work), and 10 to 15 percent slack.
- Put the hardest or most contentious work when energy is high. Put synthesis and next steps last, and protect that time.
- Mix modes: individual thinking before group discussion, small groups before plenary. This reduces anchoring on the loudest or most senior voice.
- Choose methods that fit the outcome:
  - **Diverge:** silent brainstorming, "how might we", pre-mortem.
  - **Prioritise:** impact versus feasibility grid, dot voting, a cost-of-delay ranking.
  - **Decide:** options with criteria agreed beforehand, then a vote or a decider.
  - **Align:** a map of the current process, an assumptions list.
- For hands-on AI or technical sessions, let participants build or test something real rather than watch a demo, and make sure everyone has access and accounts working before the day.

## 4. Around the session

- **Pre-work:** at most 30 minutes per participant, and only what the session cannot do without. Include what to read, bring or decide.
- **Materials and setup:** room layout, tools, templates, printouts.
- **Facilitator notes:** the likely failure modes (a dominant voice, scope creep, a missing decision-maker, a debate that restarts a settled issue) with one concrete countermeasure each, and a parking lot for off-topic points.
- **Capture:** who records, in what form, and how the outputs reach the participants within 24 hours. Name the follow-up owner.

## 5. Deliver

Give the outcome statement, the agenda table, the pre-work and the facilitator notes, in that order, on one to two pages. State assumptions at the top. Flag the one or two choices most likely to need changing once the user knows the room better.
